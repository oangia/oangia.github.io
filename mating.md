1. Central Park

Uses the setting of New York's Central Park as a framing device to illustrate human evolutionary oddities, specifically examining how our complex brains, culture, and art emerged not just for basic survival, but to attract romantic partners.


A Mind for Courtship: Argues that human mental capacities—such as humor, art, music, and morality—function primarily as courtship ornaments, analogous to a peacock's tail, designed to signal genetic quality to prospective mates.


Evolutionary Psychology Turns Dionysian: Critiques standard evolutionary psychology for being overly obsessed with practical survival (gathering, hunting) while ignoring the "Dionysian" aspects of human nature: pleasure, creativity, status-seeking, and sexual display.


Trying a Different Tool: Examines the limitations of traditional adaptationist thinking and introduces sexual selection as the missing theoretical tool needed to explain uniquely human psychological traits that defy natural selection alone.


What Makes Sexual Selection So Special?: Breaks down Charles Darwin's theory of sexual selection—operating through mate choice (epigamic selection) and intrasexual competition—and explains why it creates runaway, extravagant biological traits.


Sexual Selection and Other Forms of Social Selection: Distinguishes sexual selection from broader social selection, arguing that mate choice exerts a uniquely intense, discriminating evolutionary pressure compared to general social hierarchy or kin selection.


What Makes Sexually Selected Traits So Special?: Explores the specific criteria that define sexually selected traits, noting that they are costly to produce, functionally complex, condition-dependent, and act as reliable indicators of overall fitness.


Why Now?: Explores why evolutionary psychology is only recently equipped to tackle human sexuality and mental evolution, driven by advances in genetics, game theory, and anthropology.


The Gang of Three: Profiles the three dominant 20th-century paradigms of human nature—behaviorism, standard social science, and sociobiology—and sets the stage for a new synthesis centered on sexual selection.


Fossils, Stories, and Theories: Discusses the methodological challenges of studying mental evolution, contrasting the sparse fossil record with behavioral reconstructions and evolutionary game theory models.


Show Me the Genes: Investigates the genetic architecture required to support complex mental traits, focusing on how hundreds of thousands of genes must interact to build a sexually selected brain.


What We Can Expect From a Theory of Human Mental Evolution: Outlines the predictive power of a courtship-based theory of the mind, establishing testable hypotheses about human behavior, intelligence, and artistic output.


Working Together: Explores how mate choice acts as a collaborative, interactive evolutionary engine rather than a purely competitive one, shaping social bonding and mutual choice strategies.


An Ancestral Romance: Concludes the introductory section by reconstructing the evolutionary theater of Pleistocene courtship, portraying ancestral humans as active, discerning choosers whose aesthetic and moral preferences shaped our modern minds.


2. Darwin's Prodigy

Introduces Charles Darwin's original conceptualization of sexual selection as his most neglected yet revolutionary insight, tracing its historical rejection and eventual resurrection in evolutionary biology.


Ornaments of Gold: Examines how Darwin realized that subjective aesthetic preferences in animals could drive complex physical evolution just as efficiently as harsh environmental survival pressures.


Science by Stealth: Explores how Darwin introduced sexual selection cautiously alongside natural selection, knowing that attributing complex animal choices and tastes to evolutionary drivers would shock Victorian sensibilities.


The Grand Gateway of Sex: Details Darwin's view that sexual selection serves as a powerful engine for evolutionary divergence, speciation, and the rapid elaboration of traits.


Sexual, Natural, Artificial: Compares the mechanics of natural selection (survival through ecological pressures), artificial selection (breeder preferences), and sexual selection (mate choice).


Males Court, Females Choose: Summarizes standard sexual selection dynamics where energetic, low-investment males compete for display, while high-investment females exercise selective choice.


What Females Want: Investigates the sensory and cognitive basis of female preferences, highlighting how mate choice operates as a psychological force shaping animal ornaments and behaviors.


Darwin the Radical Psychologist: Frames Darwin as a pioneer of comparative psychology who recognized that animals possess sophisticated cognitive evaluation systems, emotions, and aesthetic senses.


Wallace Versus Female Choice: Details the fierce historical debate between Charles Darwin and Alfred Russel Wallace, noting that Wallace rejected female choice as too frivolous and anthropomorphic, preferring strict utilitarian natural selection.


Mendelian Exile: Explains how the rediscovery of Mendel's genetics temporarily pushed sexual selection out of favor, as early geneticists struggled to reconcile subjective choice with hard inheritance.


The Fisher King: Highlights Ronald Fisher's mathematical revival of sexual selection in 1930, introducing "Fisherian runaway" processes where female preferences and male traits co-evolve into exaggerated extremes.


Modern Exile: Discusses how mid-20th-century evolutionary biology (the Modern Synthesis) largely sidelined sexual selection, viewing it as a minor or non-adaptive curiosity compared to survival of the fittest.


Science Troubled by Mate Choice: Explores why mainstream science remained deeply uncomfortable with subjective mate choice, preferring mechanistic, deterministic explanations for behavior.


A Second Chance: Details the late-20th-century resurgence of sexual selection theory driven by animal behaviorists, game theorists, and mathematical modelers who validated Darwin's intuition.


The Handicap Principle Raises the Stakes: Examines Amotz Zahavi's handicap principle, which proved that costly, wasteful ornaments serve as honest, unfakable signals of superior genetic quality.


Sexual Selection Triumphant: Chronicles how sexual selection finally achieved mainstream scientific dominance in the late decades of the 20th century, completely transforming evolutionary biology.


What Sexual Selection's Exile Costs the Human Sciences: Argues that decades of ignoring sexual selection left the human behavioral sciences (psychology, sociology, anthropology) blind to the primary evolutionary drivers of human art, culture, intelligence, and morality.



3. The Runaway Brain (Evolution's Autarch): Introduces Ronald Fisher's runaway selection model as a mechanism where arbitrary aesthetic preferences and exaggerated traits feed off each other in an escalating evolutionary feedback loop.


How Runaway Works: Explains the mechanics of Fisherian runaway, showing how a genetic correlation between a preference in one sex and a display trait in the other can drive rapid, exponential evolution.


The Runaway Brain: Applies the runaway model directly to human cognitive evolution, exploring whether runaway selection could have driven our massive brains to such extreme dimensions.


The Requirements of the Runaway Process: Outlines the strict preconditions necessary for runaway selection to kick off, including genetic variation in both preference and display, and strong assortative mating.


Runaway Is Unpredictable: Emphasizes that runaway selection is fundamentally arbitrary and directionless, meaning it can produce bizarre, unpredictable evolutionary outcomes that are not strictly optimized for ecological survival.


The Problems with Runaway in Explaining the Human Mind's Evolution: Critiques the pure Fisherian model as insufficient on its own to explain human intelligence, noting that runaway traits usually lead to extreme sexual dimorphism, whereas human mental traits are largely shared between sexes.


Runaway Produces Large Sex Differences: Details how traditional runaway selection inherently generates massive physical and behavioral gaps between males and females due to asymmetric parental investment.


Eggs and Sperm: Examines biological anisogamy—the fundamental physiological divide between cheap, mobile sperm and costly, limited eggs—as the root driver of differing reproductive strategies.


Jumping Ship: Explores how genetic mutations or evolutionary shifts can cause traits or preferences to cross over between sexes, modifying standard dimorphic outcomes.


The Sexes Share Genes: Highlights the genetic constraint that human males and females share roughly 99% of their genome, meaning a runaway trait selected in one sex often drags along development in the other.


The Mental Capacities for Courtship Overlap with the Capacities for Sexual Choice: Argues that the cognitive software required to display creative courtship signals (like language, art, and wit) heavily overlaps with the cognitive software required to evaluate those same signals in a mate.


Mutual Choice: Explores how humans evolved a system of mutual mate choice—where both sexes invest heavily, display extensively, and exercise high levels of selectivity.


Assessing the Runaway Brain Theory: Evaluates the strengths and weaknesses of applying runaway selection to human mental evolution, concluding that while it explains extravagant brain size, it must be paired with indicator mechanisms (like Zahavi's handicap principle) to account for functional complexity.



4. A Mind Fit for Mating

Introduces the concept that the human brain evolved primarily as a complex fitness indicator designed to broadcast genetic health to prospective mates.


Copying Errors: Explains how mutations act as random copying errors during DNA replication, constantly threatening to degrade the genetic quality of an organism.


Mutations, Fitness, and Sexual Attractiveness: Links mutation load directly to sexual attractiveness, arguing that mates choose partners based on traits that reveal a clean, low-mutation genome.


The Human Mind as a Set of Fitness Indicators: Frames core mental faculties—such as language, creativity, and moral judgment—not as survival tools, but as costly biological advertisements of low mutation load.


Evolutionary Fitness and Physical Fitness: Contrasts everyday athletic fitness with evolutionary fitness, which measures an individual's overall genetic integrity and capacity to survive and reproduce.


Ms. Fitness USA: Examines cultural and biological perceptions of physical condition, using them as a baseline to understand how we subconsciously evaluate genetic health in human bodies.


Sexual Choice for Fitness: Details how choosy mates act as biological quality-control inspectors, selecting partners whose displays prove they carry few harmful mutations.


Why Is Fitness Still Heritable: Addresses the evolutionary paradox of why genetic variance in fitness persists despite natural selection constantly weeding out bad genes.


Time, Space, and Fitness: Explores how environmental fluctuations across time and space maintain genetic diversity by changing what constitutes an optimal fitness indicator.


The Black Rain of Mutation: Uses the metaphor of a constant, heavy downpour of deleterious mutations to describe the genetic deterioration that genomes face every generation.


The Brain as a Target for Mutation: Argues that because the brain involves thousands of interacting genes, it is exceptionally vulnerable to mutations, making it the ultimate honest test of genetic health.


How to Advertise Fitness: Explores the mechanics of honest signaling, showing how evolutionary fitness can be reliably broadcasted without being easily faked.


Advertising Within One's Budget: Explains that individuals display fitness proportional to their actual genetic health, ensuring that poor-condition individuals cannot afford high-cost signals.


An Infinite Variety of Waste: Discusses how sexual selection generates an endless array of seemingly wasteful ornaments and behaviors that serve solely as handicap-based advertisements.


Evolving Better Indicators: Traces how evolutionary arms races drive the refinement of sharper, harder-to-fake mental and physical indicators over generations.


Mental Traits as Fitness Indicators: Argues that human-specific mental traits function uniquely well as fitness indicators because intelligence, wit, and art require high metabolic and genetic investment.


The Hominid That Wasted Its Brain: Portrays ancestral humans as creatures that intentionally evolved a massive, energy-guzzling brain purely to burn surplus metabolic energy on extravagant courtship displays.


Are Fitness Indicators Immoral?: Confronts the ethical discomfort of evolutionary psychology, addressing whether viewing human virtues and mental traits as biological advertisements undercuts human dignity.
